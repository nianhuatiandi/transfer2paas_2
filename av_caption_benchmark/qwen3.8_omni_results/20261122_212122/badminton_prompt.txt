- split step — A brief unweighting or small hop followed by both feet reestablishing support before the player moves off or changes direction. Annotate from unweighting or the hop until support is reestablished. An ordinary step or change of supporting foot during a run is insufficient evidence. (Shuttle Time, pp. 40 and 51, starting footwork)
- chassé steps — The lead foot steps in the direction of travel and the trailing foot closes the gap without passing it. Group consecutive steps in the same direction and state the visible step count if discernible. Start a new event after a pause, a change of direction, or an intervening footwork pattern. A crossover step or generic sideways movement is insufficient evidence. (Shuttle Time, pp. 35 and 44, chasse steps)
- lunge — A long step in which the lead knee bends and bears weight as the torso or racket hand reaches toward the target. Annotate from the start of the step until the player begins to unload and push out of it. A lunge and a stroke can overlap. (Shuttle Time, pp. 45–46, lunging)
- lift — An upward stroke from the forecourt whose visible path or outgoing direction supports a shot toward the opponent's rear court. The shuttle may leave the frame. Report its landing point only if visible. Include the discernible striking swing and follow-through. Use a broader label if only a rising racket or a rising shuttle is visible. (Shuttle Time, pp. 54–56, backhand and forehand lift)
- net shot — A forecourt stroke in which the shuttle passes close to the top of the net and lands short or visibly descends toward the opponent's forecourt. Include the discernible striking swing and follow-through. Use trajectory evidence to distinguish it from a lift. Contact position or a gentle motion alone is insufficient. (Shuttle Time, pp. 49–51, backhand and forehand net shot)
- drive — A fast, flat shot that typically travels through midcourt on a nearly level path. Include the discernible striking swing and follow-through. Swing speed or a loud impact alone is insufficient evidence. (Shuttle Time, pp. 62–65, backhand and forehand drive)
- smash — A stroke from a high contact point that sends the shuttle sharply downward at speed. Note any visible jump in Detail. Include the discernible striking swing and follow-through. A raised racket or loud impact alone is insufficient evidence. (Shuttle Time, pp. 74–75, forehand smash)
- recovery — Movement toward an identifiable ready position after a stroke or reach, or the reestablishment of a stance for the next shot. Annotate from the exit from the preceding action until the player is ready, stops, or starts a new action. If the player uses identifiable named footwork, describe recovery as the purpose of that footwork in the same entry. (Shuttle Time, pp. 61 and 69, ready position and recovery, and Coaches' Manual Level 2, p. 80, forecourt recovery)
- backhand serve — A backhand stroke that starts a rally from the service court. Use the start-of-rally context and the visible backhand serving action. (Shuttle Time, pp. 52–53, backhand serve)
- forehand high serve — A forehand stroke that starts a rally and sends the shuttle high and deep toward the back of the receiving service court. Use both the serving context and the visible trajectory. (Shuttle Time, p. 57, forehand high serve)
- clear — A shot from the rear court toward the opponent's rear court. Use the point of contact and the deep outgoing trajectory to distinguish it from a lift played from the forecourt. (Shuttle Time, pp. 70–71, forehand overhead clear)
- drop shot — A shot from the rear court that descends into the opponent's forecourt. Use the rear-court contact point and the short trajectory to distinguish it from a net shot. (Shuttle Time, pp. 72–73, forehand overhead drop shot)
- backhand block — A defensive backhand return that redirects an incoming attack toward the opponent's forecourt or midcourt. Look for the incoming shot, defensive contact, and the redirected path. (Shuttle Time, pp. 76–77, backhand block off the body)
- scissor jump — A rear-court jump with visible body rotation and an exchange of the legs' forward and backward positions. Annotate the jump from takeoff through landing. A jump and a stroke can overlap. (Shuttle Time, p. 69, scissor jump)
